On **October 23, 2026**, the DKU Computer Science Club ran a two-and-a-half-hour session in **AB 2103** on the fields inside computing, the graduate programmes that lead into them, and the employers that hire from them. The session was in English and open to every year group.

The premise was narrow. Three in ten DKU graduates — 260 of 871 — go on to a master's in computer science, AI or information technology, the largest single destination for the graduating class. Most of those students have to choose a direction inside that number, and the choice is not "computer science or not". It is nineteen directions that share a name and very little else.

## Nineteen directions, five groups

The first part of the session was a field map. The nineteen directions were sorted into five groups:

- **AI & Learning** — machine learning, natural language processing and large language models, computer vision, robotics, and trustworthy AI;
- **Data Science & Engineering** — data science and analytics, and data engineering;
- **Systems & Engineering** — computer systems, software engineering and programming languages, security and privacy, and AI infrastructure;
- **Applied Mathematics & Algorithms** — theory, optimisation and operations research, scientific computing, and quantum computing;
- **Interdisciplinary & Applied** — fintech, quantitative finance, human-computer interaction, and computational biology.

![The field map shown at the session](/images/blog/cs-fields-2026/map.webp)

Each direction received two pages. The first was a card: a one-sentence definition, the areas the field is actually made of, the job titles it leads to, and examples of employers. The second answered a different question — what the work involves — in four concrete tasks, from preparing data and a baseline in machine learning to making sure a payment retry does not charge twice in fintech.

Each group closed with a page of master's programmes: Carnegie Mellon, Stanford, ETH Zurich, Oxford, UC Berkeley, Duke and others, with a note on what kind of degree each one is. Two appendices, kept out of the main flow, list the leading research groups and the venues where work in each field is published.

## The field map, block by block

The map was presented by seven club members, each taking a run of consecutive directions. What follows is every block in the order it was given, with the card that opened it.

### Fanqi Shi

**Machine Learning · Computer Vision · Robotics & Autonomous Systems**

![Fanqi Shi presenting Computer Vision](/images/blog/cs-fields-2026/speakers/fanqi-shi.webp)

Fanqi Shi took the first three directions in AI & Learning.

**Machine learning** is learning patterns from data or experience and testing whether the model still works on samples it has never seen; generalisation is the point, not fit on the training set. The card listed deep learning, optimisation, learning theory, probabilistic models, generative models and representation learning as its key areas. The work page followed one model from a baseline through training and tuning one change at a time, error analysis and ablations to find whether the limit is data, capacity or optimisation, and deployment with monitoring for distribution shift.

**Computer vision** recognises content, position, shape and change in images, video and 3D scenes under different lighting, viewpoints and occlusion. The work starts with the dataset — class balance, lighting, viewpoints and rare cases decide what the model can learn — then fine-tunes a pretrained backbone, evaluates per scenario because average accuracy hides failure on small or occluded objects, and deploys under latency and hardware constraints.

**Robotics and autonomous systems** perceive the environment, plan an action and carry it out in the real world, with feedback closing the loop. Its key areas run from perception and estimation to planning and control, manipulation and locomotion, embodied AI and autonomous vehicles. The work page covered state estimation from noisy sensors, trajectory planning and control, contact under forces that are hard to model, and the transfer from simulation to hardware.

![The Machine Learning card](/images/blog/cs-fields-2026/slides/a1-machine-learning.webp)

### Zihan Zhang

**Natural Language Processing & Large Language Models · Trustworthy AI, Safety & Ethics**

![Zihan Zhang presenting Trustworthy AI, Safety & Ethics](/images/blog/cs-fields-2026/speakers/zihan-zhang.webp)

Zihan Zhang closed the group with its other two directions.

**NLP and large language models** generate, retrieve, translate and act on language — pretrained on large text corpora, then adapted to a task. The work page was blunt about where the effort sits: instruction and preference data shape behaviour, and most teams never pretrain from scratch; retrieval grounds answers in evidence; evaluation covers the whole application, including safety, latency and cost; and batching, caching and quantisation decide the price of each answer.

**Trustworthy AI, safety and ethics** asks whether a system is reliable in its real context, not only accurate on a test set, and whether it can be audited, challenged and governed. Its key areas are interpretability, fairness and bias, robustness, safety and alignment, privacy, and governance and standards. The work is to report error rates for each population rather than the average, test whether an explanation is faithful, stress-test and red-team against perturbations, jailbreaks and misuse, and carry risk assessment, human oversight and incident response across the lifecycle.

![The Natural Language Processing & LLMs card](/images/blog/cs-fields-2026/slides/a2-nlp-llms.webp)

### Yichen Shen

**Data Science & Analytics · Data Engineering & Data Systems**

![Yichen Shen presenting Data Science & Analytics](/images/blog/cs-fields-2026/speakers/yichen-shen.webp)

Yichen Shen took the two directions in Data Science & Engineering.

**Data science and analytics** turns data into inference, prediction and a decision; statistics supplies the reasoning about uncertainty and computing the machinery. The work page begins with the estimand — the population and the decision the result will inform — then the measurement design, an effect with an error bar, and a report that carries the assumptions and the uncertainty to the person who acts on it.

**Data engineering** is the systems that collect, store, query, move and serve data — reliably, at volume, and at a cost someone will pay. The work is the schema, storage layout and file format chosen for the read pattern; a pipeline with quality checks and lineage for every table; transactions, recovery and exactly-once semantics under retries and failure; and a workload report behind any speed claim.

![The Data Science & Analytics card](/images/blog/cs-fields-2026/slides/b1-data-science.webp)

### He Yi

**Computer Systems · Software Engineering & Programming Languages · Security & Privacy · AI Infrastructure**

![He Yi presenting Computer Systems](/images/blog/cs-fields-2026/speakers/he-yi.webp)

He Yi took all four directions in Systems & Engineering.

**Computer systems** covers how hardware, operating systems, networks and storage work together to run applications, from one machine to a datacentre. The work page was a warning against guessing: profile first, because the bottleneck is usually memory, I/O or the network rather than the code that looks slow; choose the abstraction the system can promise; implement against the hardware; then validate under failure and tail latency at the scale the system will actually see.

**Software engineering and programming languages** is how software is specified, built, tested and maintained, and how languages make that possible or hard. The work is to state a property through a type, an invariant or a formal specification, build the analysis or tool that checks it, fuzz for the failures the analysis missed, and match the evidence to the claim.

**Security and privacy** protects computation, communication and personal data against adversaries, starting from a threat model that states what the attacker can observe, control or compromise. The work is to find the weakness through fuzzing, static analysis, symbolic execution or review, build the defence from isolation, privilege separation and cryptography, then measure it and disclose through coordinated disclosure.

**AI infrastructure** is the systems that train, serve and operate models at scale — cluster scheduling, distributed training, interconnect and inference serving. Its key areas include hardware co-design and platform operations. The work is to profile every layer, parallelise training with communication overlapped with compute, serve within a latency and cost budget, and operate with checkpointing, elastic restart and cost per token reported.

![The Computer Systems card](/images/blog/cs-fields-2026/slides/c1-computer-systems.webp)

Hanyue Deng took the first two directions in Applied Mathematics & Algorithms.

**Theory of computing and algorithms** asks what can be computed at all, how fast and with how much memory, and what must be proved before an answer counts. Its key areas run from algorithms and complexity theory to approximation and randomness, algorithmic game theory, and online and streaming algorithms. The work page kept correctness and cost apart: a correctness argument and a running-time or lower bound, each proved under a stated model, then tested against real instances.

**Optimisation and operations research** finds a good or optimal decision under an objective and constraints — continuous or discrete, with a bound on quality. The work is to turn a messy requirement into decision variables, constraints and an objective; match the method to the structure; report a dual bound or optimality gap rather than just a feasible answer; and check the solution against the assumptions and requirements the model left out.

### Haohong Yu

**Scientific Computing & Numerical Analysis · Quantum Computing & Information**

![Haohong Yu presenting Quantum Computing & Information](/images/blog/cs-fields-2026/speakers/haohong-yu.webp)

Haohong Yu took the last two directions in the group.

**Scientific computing and numerical analysis** makes mathematical models computable in finite precision, and analyses the approximation, round-off and stability that follow. The work is to discretise the model, choose the solver and preconditioner for the matrix structure, analyse the error rather than compare a few step sizes, and run at scale across the memory hierarchy and GPU kernels.

**Quantum computing and information** uses quantum states and operations as computational resources, where any advantage depends on the task, model and hardware. Its key areas run from quantum algorithms and error correction to quantum hardware, quantum information theory and post-quantum security. The work is to state the model behind a claimed advantage, design the algorithm, error-correcting code or control sequence, count qubits and depth plus the classical cost of preparing data and decoding, and validate on a simulator before a device.

![The Scientific Computing & Numerical Analysis card](/images/blog/cs-fields-2026/slides/d3-scientific-computing.webp)

### Wenxin Xu

**FinTech · Quantitative Finance · Human-Computer Interaction · Computational Biology & Bioinformatics**

![Wenxin Xu presenting Quantitative Finance](/images/blog/cs-fields-2026/speakers/wenxin-xu.webp)

Wenxin Xu closed the field map with the four directions in Interdisciplinary & Applied.

**FinTech** combines finance's domain knowledge with computer science: the systems that move, lend, insure and account for money. The work page set the standard as exactness, auditability and availability — ledgers and settlement that reconcile under retries and partial failure, risk checks inside a latency budget, append-only records and reproducible reports, and reporting to the deadline rather than as a back-office afterthought.

**Quantitative finance** turns uncertain market prices into estimates of value, risk and a decision — pricing, hedging and position sizing. Its key areas are derivatives pricing and hedging, risk measurement, portfolio construction, numerical methods, optimisation under constraints and statistical learning for markets. The work is to write the model and its assumptions down, price the same product two ways against a closed-form value, measure value at risk, expected shortfall and stress scenarios, and backtest with transaction costs before trading.

**Human-computer interaction** designs, builds and evaluates technology around people, asking whether they can learn it, trust it and recover when it fails. The work starts from what the user is trying to complete, prototypes at the cheapest fidelity that answers the question, tests with a few participants on a real task, and judges against task completion, time on task, error rate or a standardised score.

**Computational biology and bioinformatics** uses computation to find patterns in biological data and make predictions that experiments can then test. The work is to form the question, process sequencing, structure and image data with quality control and batch effects handled, predict with the uncertainty reported, and validate independently in another cohort or in the lab.

![The FinTech card](/images/blog/cs-fields-2026/fintech.webp)

## The second half: a conversation, not a lecture

The field map answers what the directions are. It does not answer how a student moves from one to the next, which is why the second part of the session was a conversation with **Allen Guangzhi Su**.

Allen graduated from DKU in 2026 in **Applied Mathematics and Computational Science (Computer Science)**. He was founding president of the DKU Finance and CS Club, worked as a research assistant in Duke's Center for Computational Evolutionary Intelligence, and interned at TikTok as an AI product manager. He is now in Carnegie Mellon's MS in Artificial Intelligence and Innovation, having also held offers from NYU's fully funded Data Science PhD, CMU's Electrical and Computer Engineering programme, Northwestern's MS in AI, and Cornell's information systems programme with a health-technology focus.

![Tianrui Gu hosting the conversation with Allen Guangzhi Su](/images/blog/cs-fields-2026/speakers/allen-conversation.webp)

The conversation was hosted by **Tianrui Gu** and joined by Allen over video. The questions were prepared in advance and grouped in three rounds, each one step further from the syllabus: what Allen did and would do again, how a student gets into research, and what none of it covers. Each question named a concrete case — the programme, the employer, the year — rather than asking for general advice, which is what made the answers specific. The prepared rounds were followed by questions from the room.

### Round one · Background and internships

**Which master's programme at CMU did you choose, and why?**

He compared the offers on the same four things: what the coursework actually teaches, which group and supervisor a student would work with, how long the degree runs, and what it costs. The PhD at NYU was the research route and fully funded; CMU's Electrical and Computer Engineering and Northwestern's MS in AI were broader engineering degrees; Cornell's programme led toward health technology. He chose the MS in Artificial Intelligence and Innovation because the courses and the project work at the end matched the work he wants to do — building AI products — rather than because of the title. Read the module list and find the group you would join; the name of the programme is the least informative part of the offer.

**With ByteDance on your CV: how should an undergraduate think about internships — is it too early to rush, is one necessary, and which fields expect one?**

His own internship at ByteDance was on the product side of AI, not the training side: what users ask for, what a model can actually do, and where the gap between the two is. On timing, his answer was that it is not too early once you have something to show, and it is too early when you have nothing — the first internship is found on evidence, and the evidence is a project, a course result or a piece of research you can describe. Whether one is necessary depends on the field. In quantitative finance the internship is the entry point; in research it supplements a group and a paper; in software engineering it is the standard route in. The mistake is collecting internships as logos instead of using each one to test whether you want the work.

### Round two · Research and professors

**You completed two research projects as an undergraduate. What were they, and what is undergraduate research for?**

The two projects came up as examples rather than headlines, and what they taught was the sequence: read enough to find a question that is still open, cut it down to something a student can finish, and report the result including the part that failed. Undergraduate research is not mainly a publication machine — it is the first place where the work has no syllabus. It also gives a student something concrete to talk about in interviews and applications, which is the practical return on the time.

**For a first- or second-year student, is it better to get the courses right first, or to reach out and learn by doing?**

The order matters more than the choice. The coursework gives you the vocabulary to be useful in a group, and reaching out before that produces a conversation neither side can sustain. For a first- or second-year, his answer was to get the core right — algorithms, mathematics, systems — and to reach out as soon as there is a specific question you cannot answer alone. Learning by doing is not the alternative to coursework; it is what you do with it once you have it.

**How do you build a working relationship with a professor?**

Read the professor's papers before the first meeting, arrive with a question about the work rather than a request for a position, and do the first small task well and on time. The relationship is built the way other working relationships are: reliability on small things, honesty about what you do not know, and early warning when something is stuck. A professor who has to chase a student stops giving that student work.

**Most first- and second-years have no research experience. How do you join a group anyway?**

Join by doing something small first — a replication of a result from the group's reading, a clean implementation of a method from one of their papers, or a contribution to the group's code. Groups take students who have already finished something, because the first task is a test of whether more work is worth handing over. The missing research experience is not the obstacle; the missing evidence that you can finish something is.

### Round three · The field, the portfolio and people

**The field moves fast and AI keeps getting stronger. Do programming languages, algorithms, low-level systems and mathematics still matter as much?**

He answered with two slides he had prepared for the room, and they are two halves of one answer.

![Allen's slide: What You Must Do in the AI Era — use AI tools](/images/blog/cs-fields-2026/slides/allen-slide-1.webp)

The first is about using the tools: build a full-stack app or website with Codex, produce a polished presentation with Claude, prepare and submit a resume with Muse. The joke on the slide — writing an assignment with ChatGPT — is the point: a tool can produce the artefact, but not the judgement about whether the artefact should exist.

![Allen's slide: Build Strong Foundations](/images/blog/cs-fields-2026/slides/allen-slide-2.webp)

The second is about what the tools stand on: Python syntax and programming habits, the key libraries in NumPy, Pandas and Hugging Face, the new concepts such as recursive self-improvement and GUI agents, and enough curiosity to try the products as they appear. The fundamentals did not become optional when the tools got better. They became the thing that lets you tell a good output from a plausible one.

**Should a CS student build a portfolio and contribute to open source? How much does either count for jobs and for research?**

A portfolio is evidence, so the question is evidence of what. One finished system — with a baseline, a result and the failure you report honestly — is worth more than a list of tutorials, and the deck's closing advice said the same. Open source adds a second kind of evidence: that you can read and change code someone else wrote, and answer a reviewer. It counts most where the job is building software and less in research, where a paper or a group is the currency; but a merged patch is legible in both.

**What did organising club activities give you? Are leadership, communication and working with people — the skills CS students often neglect — more important in the age of AI?**

Organising the club gave him the part of the work no problem set contains: setting an agenda, persuading people to come, delegating, and being accountable when something fails in public. His view was that these skills become more valuable as AI systems get stronger, not less. When the artefact is cheap to produce, the scarce part is deciding what to build, explaining why it matters, and getting other people to move with you. The technical baseline is being compressed; judgement and communication are not.

### Questions from the room

When the prepared rounds ended, the session opened to the room. Two questions came up that the deck had only implied.

**How should a DKU student choose courses at Duke?**

Choose by the group and the syllabus, not by the course code: read what the module covers, who teaches it and how it is assessed, and take the core course of a field early enough to find out whether the field is for you. A schedule built from safe grades teaches less than one built from the courses a direction actually rests on, and the electives are where a direction gets tested. The useful question about a course is not whether it is hard, but what you build in it.

**What does all-round development look like during the undergraduate years?**

He treated it as evidence rather than a checklist: a piece of research, an internship, a club you had to run, a project you finished. Each tests a different part of the work — research whether you can find a question, an internship whether you can deliver inside someone else's process, a club whether you can get other people to move. One or two done well are worth more than five done for the line on a CV, provided you can say what each one taught you.

The room's questions got the same treatment as the prepared ones: a concrete case, a specific answer.

## What the session left students with

The deck was deliberately long on contrast and short on prescription. Its closing advice was to leave with one direction and one concrete next step for the year, not with nineteen half-remembered field names: take the field's core course early, build one small system end to end, read one paper a week from the field's main venue, and apply for an internship once there is work to show.

The session slides are available from the club, and the field cards, programme tables, research groups and publication venues remain available for students who want to compare directions after the event.
